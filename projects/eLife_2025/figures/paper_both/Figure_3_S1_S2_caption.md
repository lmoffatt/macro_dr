# Figure 3—figure supplement 1 caption

**Figure 3—figure supplement 1. Per-step information against score variance, for every member and all four identified parameters.**

All seven members of the lattice across the columns in cost-ladder order, and the four parameters the recording identifies down the page as bands of two rows each: the opening rate k_on (A, B), the closing rate k_off (C, D), the unitary current i (E, F) and the channel number N_ch (G, H). Same 1,000 recordings at the same cell as Figure 3 (N_ch = 100, noise label 0.1, Δ·k_off = 0.1), scored with no fitting; grey marks the agonist pulse, time in milliseconds.

The upper row of each band carries the two magnitudes, on a logarithmic axis and against no reference: the per-interval Gaussian Fisher information F_t = E[I_t] in blue and the score variance J_t = Var(s_t) in orange. They lie on each other wherever the member is calibrated at that interval. The lower row is the test made from them, log₁₀(J_t/F_t) with its 95% bootstrap interval over the recordings, zero for a per-interval-calibrated member. Curves stop where the information reaches the mask at 10⁻⁶, an absolute floor rather than a fraction of the peak, and the axis of the upper row ends at that same floor, so a curve that falls to it is seen to terminate rather than to be cut off.

**Per-interval calibration is not monotone along the cost ladder, and that is what the seven columns are for.** The median of log₁₀(J_t/F_t) over the informative intervals reads, on k_off: +0.184 for LSE, −0.038 for NR, **+0.007 for INR**, −0.145 for R, −0.229 for MR, −0.135 for VR and +0.033 for IR. Counting instead the fraction of informative intervals whose interval covers zero, INR reads 0.79, 0.76, 0.73 and 0.79 across the four parameters against IR's 0.93, 0.70, 0.94 and 0.96, while the three recursive members between them read 0.00 to 0.30. The cheapest member above least squares that models the interval average is better calibrated interval by interval than any of the three recursive members that cost more, and only the boundary-conditioned filter is better.

**What that member does with the calibration it has.** INR is the one place in the paper where the two levels of the diagnostic come apart cleanly. It passes every check that can be made one interval at a time: its mean squared standardized residual is 0.9995 against IR's 0.9989, its interval excludes one at none of the intervals, its per-interval information ratio is the one above, and its score bias sits near the chance level (Figure 3—figure supplement 2). Its accumulated information ratio is 20.8 and its lag-one score autocorrelation 0.87. So a likelihood can report an honest predictive distribution and an honest information at every single interval and still misreport the information in the whole recording by a factor of twenty. The failure is in the accumulation and nowhere else, which is what conditioning on both interval ends repairs.

**Where each parameter is measured, and for how long.** During the agonist pulse all four are measured, and the two amplitude parameters carry nearly the same information, so their curves overlie (for IR, mean F_t of 62.4 for i against 55.1 for N_ch). At the washout they separate into two pairs. The information about k_on and about N_ch collapses to the numerical floor within six intervals: for IR the channel-number information runs 55.4, 10.5, 0.357, 1.67×10⁻², 6.9×10⁻⁴, 2.8×10⁻⁵, 1.05×10⁻⁶. The other two survive the relaxation: over the last ten intervals IR reads 1.13 for k_off and 0.659 for i, against 1.4×10⁻²¹ for N_ch and 7.9×10⁻⁴⁴ for k_on.

The two survivals are not of the same kind, and the decomposition F_t = (∂μ/∂θ)²/v + (∂v/∂θ)²/2v² says how they differ. Over those last ten intervals 81% of what k_off keeps comes from the mean, the shape of the decay its own kinetics govern, while 71% of what i keeps comes from the variance, the gating fluctuation of the few channels still open. For N_ch the information is 99.6% mean-borne, and it is that mean derivative which vanishes: once the filter has conditioned on the past, its predicted current is the open count it already carries times i, and no longer depends on how many channels the patch holds. The collapse is therefore a property of the conditioned members; the open-loop columns retain 15 to 16% of their pulse information about N_ch through the washout, against 0.5 to 0.8% for R and IR.

**Least squares fails already here, and in a different way.** Its ratio row is the same curve in all four bands, with a median of 0.183889 in every one. That is its constant-variance premise showing itself rather than a rendering fault: with one variance the per-interval score is (y − μ)(∂μ/∂θ)/σ² and its information is (∂μ/∂θ)²/σ², so the parameter cancels out of the ratio and what is left is the true residual variance over the assumed one. The curve therefore breathes with the gating, above zero through the transient where the real variance exceeds the flat plug-in and below it in the tail. This is the heteroscedasticity of Figure 3's residual row, read at the level of the score, and it is a per-interval failure where the recursive family's is cross-time.

**The channel-number band carries no least-squares panel**, by exclusion and not by absence of data. Least squares does not model the gating fluctuations, which are the only thing separating the channel count from the unitary current, so with them unmodelled the two enter only through their product: F_t over the pulse is 1076.55 for both, to every digit. The same exclusion is applied in Figure 3 and, through EXCLUDE_ROWS, in the Figure 4 family.
