I am submitting “Likelihood approximations distort the ion channel kinetic information in macroscopic currents” for consideration as a Research Article.

Macroscopic currents are routinely used to infer the kinetic mechanisms of ion channels. Yet a likelihood can fit such recordings well without reporting the information that the recordings actually contain. This creates a failure mode that ordinary goodness-of-fit, posterior diagnostics, and visual agreement cannot detect: the inferred parameters may be precise, and the model may reproduce the current, while the likelihood nevertheless assigns the wrong amount of information to the data.

This problem became concrete for us in the study of P2X2 receptors. In 2007, we proposed a kinetic state from a feature that could be read directly from the current: a delay before activation that was subsequently confirmed. The mechanistic questions now being asked of these receptors are different. Allosteric coupling between subunits and asymmetries in that coupling leave no comparably direct signature in a macroscopic current, so their inference depends on distinguishing kinetic schemes through their statistical evidence. In our recent P2X2 work, the mechanistic conclusion depended on ratios of evidence between nine schemes. Replacing the likelihood with a control differing only in its recursion changed the leading mechanism and eliminated a margin of roughly six in log evidence. We reported the sensitivity and argued for one arm of the mechanism, but the recording itself provides no ground truth against which to decide which likelihood has reported the correct information.

We therefore developed a direct validation framework for likelihoods of macroscopic currents. Rather than asking whether a fitted model reproduces the data, we ask whether the likelihood itself satisfies two identities that must hold when it represents the stated statistical information: the score must average to zero, and its covariance must equal the Fisher information. Exact simulation of channel gating allows these identities to be tested at known parameters, independently of whether the likelihood can itself be evaluated exactly. We applied this test to eight likelihoods, from least squares to a filter that conditions each interval average on both endpoints, across 210 combinations of channel number, noise, and sampling interval in a two-state channel. Because the generating process is known, the discrepancy measures the approximation's own error rather than model misspecification.

The result is direct. One commonly used recursive approximation accumulates approximately twenty times the information actually supported by a record, whereas the recursion in the calibrated formulation restores the information to the correct scale. This is not merely a numerical discrepancy: it changes the evidential comparison between kinetic mechanisms. We do not recompute the P2X2 evidences here. Instead, we show that the likelihood responsible for the earlier change in ranking is already distorted in the simplest possible model, while the calibrated likelihood is not. The distortion is localized in the low-channel regime, occurs in both directions, and can be mapped as a function of noise and sampling interval.

Our work also establishes a complementary validation principle to posterior coverage testing. Münch and colleagues used coverage to assess a filtering approach in eLife in 2022. Coverage tests whether an inference procedure produces calibrated posteriors, but require a complete fit for each replicate. The identities we use test the likelihood itself in a single pass and return a parameter-resolved diagnostic showing both what information is misreported and whether the error arises locally at each sample or accumulates across the record. Our calibrated likelihood agrees to one part in 10^8 with an integrated-measurement Kalman filter established previously, providing an independent check on the implementation. The methodological advance is therefore not another filtering scheme, but a way to establish whether an approximation preserves the information of a macroscopic measurement.

The resulting map has a simple implication for experimental interpretation: there is no region in which both a trustworthy least-squares error bar and reliable information about the unitary current are available, and the corresponding boundaries differ by one to three decades in noise. The recordings that are sufficiently rich to determine current amplitudes are therefore precisely those in which classical error estimates can have the wrong width.

The calibrated likelihood, its score and Fisher information, the exact simulator, and the distortion diagnostic are provided as a small library with R and Python bindings. Researchers can therefore apply the same validation to their own kinetic schemes rather than having to assume that a likelihood is informative merely because it fits the current.

We believe this work will be of broad interest to eLife because it addresses a fundamental but largely invisible assumption in mechanistic inference from macroscopic recordings: that a likelihood which reproduces a measurement also preserves the information contained in that measurement. We show that this assumption can fail systematically, provide a direct test for the failure, and identify when it matters for biological conclusions.
