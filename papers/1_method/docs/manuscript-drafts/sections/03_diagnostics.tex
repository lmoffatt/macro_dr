\section*{Diagnostics: testing a likelihood against its simulation}

Everything below is read off ensembles of exactly simulated recordings, computed at two parameter vectors, and every figure states which one it shows. The simulation truth $\theta_{\mathrm{sim}}$ is the point the recordings were generated from, never known in an experiment; the pooled optimum $\theta_{\mathrm{pool}}$ is the maximum-likelihood fit of the member under test to all the recordings of one cell at once, located numerically (Methods). Their difference $\theta_{\mathrm{pool}}-\theta_{\mathrm{sim}}$, which correct specification would leave at sampling error, is the misspecification bias itself. The two anchors answer different questions: at $\theta_{\mathrm{sim}}$ a nonzero mean score is visible at all, while at $\theta_{\mathrm{pool}}$ the score vanishes by construction, so the information comparison is not contaminated by a displaced gradient. % src: sections/06_methods.tex "Parameter estimation and the two anchors" (optimiser settings, warm start, near-zero gradient certificate)

Three quantities are read off the simulation, in increasing strength. The first is the standardized residual $r_t=(y_t-\mu_t)/\sigma_t$, with $\mu_t$ and $\sigma_t^2$ the likelihood's own predictive mean and variance for interval $t$: mean zero, unit variance and no autocorrelation across intervals if the one-step predictive distribution is correct \emph{at the parameters it is evaluated at} (Figure~\ref{fig:time} draws the second and the third). It is the only one of the three an experimentalist can run on a real recording, needing no ensemble and no known truth, and it does need a fitted point, since away from one the properties fail for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the approximation is any good. A fit with the baseline and the noise level free absorbs most of the first two properties; the temporal structure is what no fit absorbs. Residual whiteness is therefore the part that stays informative at the optimum, and it is a conservative reading of what the strongest check quantifies, running $1.6$ to $1.7$ times smaller than the score autocorrelation for the members in which either is large. % src: projects/eLife_2025/figures/paper_both/figure_3.html (row G caption numbers; lag-one residual and score ACF by member: LSE 0.846/0.856, NR 0.510/0.864, R 0.118/0.191, IR -0.010/-0.004). The first residual moment is Figure 3--figure supplement 3 row A, cited from Results so that the three Figure 3 supplements are cited in ascending order.

The second is the score. With $\ell(\theta)$ the total log-likelihood and $S(\theta)=\nabla_\theta\ell(\theta)$ its gradient, correct specification requires $\mathbb{E}[S(\theta_{\mathrm{sim}})]=0$ at the simulation truth. Projected through the parameter covariance, a nonzero mean score becomes a displacement in parameter units, $b=\mathbf{H}^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}[S]$, with $\mathbf{H}$ the Gaussian Fisher information the likelihood itself reports (Eq.~\ref{eq:gaussian-fisher} below), which is how far the estimate is pushed (Figure~\ref{fig:time}). This one cannot be run on an experiment at all: it needs a known truth and an ensemble to take the expectation over, and at the only point an experimenter has, their own fit, the score vanishes by construction.

The third and strongest is the information itself. Correct specification requires $\mathrm{Cov}[S]=\mathbf{H}$, the covariance taken over independent recordings of the same cell, which is what no experiment supplies: one recording gives one score. An approximation can pass the residual and score checks and still violate this one by an order of magnitude. Because the failure is directional, we record it as a matrix,
\[ \mathbf{C}=\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{J}\,\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}, \]
with $\mathbf{J}=\mathrm{Cov}(S)$ the covariance of the total score, cross-interval terms included, so that $\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}$ exactly under correct specification. This is the classical information-matrix-equality comparison \cite{white1982maximum}. In the classical setting the two Bartlett identities can only be tested, through a statistic with a known finite-sample defect, because the data-generating law is unknown; here they are measured, and what is new is reading the output as a measured property of each approximation rather than as a hypothesis to accept or reject.

We fix the direction by convention: $C_{ii}>1$ means the likelihood under-reports the uncertainty in parameter $i$ and is over-confident, $C_{ii}<1$ means it over-reports and is conservative.

The anchor $\mathbf{H}$ is the likelihood's own Gaussian Fisher information,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{H}=\sum_t\mathbb{E}\!\left[
    \frac{(\nabla\mu_t)(\nabla\mu_t)^\top}{\sigma_t^2}
    +\frac{(\nabla\sigma_t^2)(\nabla\sigma_t^2)^\top}{2\sigma_t^4}\right],
  \label{eq:gaussian-fisher}
\end{equation}
formed from the predictive moments and their parameter derivatives that the score already requires (Methods). For a macroscopic algorithm the likelihood is Gaussian by construction, so this is the model's own Fisher and $\mathbf{C}$ tests the equality in the model's own frame. The comparison is not circular: $\mathbf{H}$ is the information the likelihood claims and $\mathbf{J}$ the information it delivers against data from the true process.

All three checks run on the whole cost ladder set out in Theory, from least squares at the bottom, in both of its window settings, to the boundary-conditioned filter at the top. The bottom rung needs a qualification, because its predictive variance is not a gating quantity: least squares holds one variance constant over the recording, so what it reports is $\mathbf{H}$ above with $\sigma_t^2$ replaced by that constant, for both arms alike, and averaging the predicted mean over the interval leaves the premise untouched. The exact process violates it in every cell we simulate, since the variance of a recorded interval rises and falls with the open population and with the channel number. For a least-squares arm, then, $\mathrm{Cov}(S)$ read against the reported information measures the size of that break rather than a failure of the same kind as a gating-aware likelihood's, where the predictive variance does follow the gating; the Results quote the least-squares ratio with that reading and do not treat the two as one comparable quantity.

The same matrix repairs what it measures. The sandwich covariance \cite{huber1967behavior,godambe1960optimum}
\[ \Sigma=\mathbf{H}^{-1}\,\mathbf{J}\,\mathbf{H}^{-1}=\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{H}^{-1/2} \]
substitutes the information the likelihood actually carries for the information it reported. It and the bias vector $b$ are first-order approximations, from a Taylor expansion of the score at the optimum with the empirical Hessian replaced by its expectation $-\mathbf{H}$ (see Supplementary Information). We verify it directly, without leaning on the expansion, against the empirical covariance of the estimates over the simulated recordings (Figure~\ref{fig:clouds}).

The distortion splits into two physically distinct parts (Figure~\ref{fig:plane}--figure supplement 1).

The sample distortion $\mathbf{C}_s=\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{J}_s\,\mathbf{H}^{-1/2}$, built from the within-interval score covariance $\mathbf{J}_s=\sum_t\mathrm{Cov}(s_t)$ with $s_t=\nabla_\theta\ell_t$ the score of interval $t$, measures per-sample departure from Gaussianity, to the leading corrections the third and fourth cumulants of the interval current. The correlation distortion $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{J}_s^{-1/2}\,\mathbf{J}\,\mathbf{J}_s^{-1/2}$ measures the cross-interval correlation of the score, which $\mathbf{J}_s$ misses and which is exactly the local time correlation of the current that \cite{milescu2005maximum} named as the dominant error of non-recursive fitting; $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ when the score carries no cross-interval dependence. One parameter at a time, $F_t$ is the Gaussian Fisher information of interval $t$ alone, so that the corresponding diagonal entry of $\mathbf{H}$ is $\sum_t F_t$, and $J_t=\mathrm{Var}(s_t)$; accumulated, $F_T=\sum_{t\le T}F_t$ and $J_T=\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\sum_{t\le T}s_t\bigr)$, and it is $J_T$, not the sum of the $J_t$, that the reported information has to match, so $J_t=F_t$ and $J_T=F_T$ are not equivalent.

The correlation part has a reader-facing translation. For an additive statistic $X_t$ with sum $L=\sum_t X_t$, the ratio $\kappa=\mathrm{Var}(L)\big/\sum_t\mathrm{Var}(X_t)$ is one when the terms are independent and larger when they are positively correlated, and it converts to an effective sample size $T_\mathrm{eff}=T/\kappa$: a non-recursive likelihood believes it has $T$ independent intervals when it effectively has $T/\kappa$. Where $\mathbf{R}\approx\kappa\mathbf{I}$ that scalar captures the temporal error; where $\mathbf{R}$ is anisotropic, or $\mathbf{C}_s\neq\mathbf{I}$, no sample-size correction suffices and the full matrix is needed.

A matrix is not a number, and two scalars summarise it without pretending to replace it. With $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d$ the
eigenvalues of $\mathbf{C}$ on the retained subspace, $\bar e = d^{-1}\sum_i \log\lambda_i$ the mean
of their logarithms and $s^2 = \mathrm{Var}_i(\log\lambda_i)$ the variance, the two reported
throughout are the exponentials
\begin{equation}
  m \;=\; e^{\bar e} \;=\; \Bigl(\textstyle\prod_i \lambda_i\Bigr)^{1/d},
  \qquad
  a \;=\; e^{\,s},
  \label{eq:magnitude-anisotropy}
\end{equation}
so that $m$ is the typical factor by which the reported information is wrong, the geometric mean of
the per-direction factors, and $a$ is their multiplicative spread from direction to direction, both
one at correct specification. Their single-scalar combination is the affine-invariant Riemannian
distance from the identity,
$D_{\mathrm{AI}} = \bigl(\sum_i \log^2\lambda_i\bigr)^{1/2} = \sqrt{d\,(\bar e^2 + s^2)}$, reported
nowhere in place of the pair. In error-bar units take the square root of any of these, since the
distortion is a ratio of informations: $1.15$ is a $7\%$ error on a confidence interval, $2$ is
$41\%$, and $4$ turns a nominal $95\%$ interval into about $68\%$.
